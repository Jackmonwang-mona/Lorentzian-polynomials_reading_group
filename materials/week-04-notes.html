General content of Marcus' part

Today I've already presented all of Chapter 2.1. The last theorem we finished was Theorem 2.13: the closure of strictly Lorentzian polynomials in \((H_n^d)\) contains Lorentzian polynomials.

Our first short term goal is Thm 2.25: The closure of strictly Lorentzian polynomials in \((H_n^d)\) is Lorentzian polynomials.

General content of Ivy's part

We break the proof of \(L_n^d\) being closed into two main pieces:

  • Proving that polynomials in \(L_n^d\) are \(c\)-Rayleigh (Prop 2.19).
  • Proving that being homogeneous and \(c\)-Rayleigh implies having an \(M\)-convex support (Theorem 2.23).

We successfully prove the first part. Here is a quick breakdown of how we got there:

  • By looking at the \(d=2\) case, we found that if the Hessian matrix has exactly one positive eigenvalue, we directly get the \(1\)-Rayleigh property.
  • The same logic generalizes perfectly to arbitrary dimensions to yield the \(2(1-1/d)\)-Rayleigh property. Thus, the problem reduces to proving that the Hessian of any polynomial in \(L_n^d\) has exactly one positive eigenvalue (Thm 2.16(2)). The homotopy defined in Thm 2.13 implies that \(\mathring{L}_n^d\) is connected, and thus we only needed to show two things:
    1. The Hessian is nonsingular everywhere in \(\mathring{L}_n^d\) (Thm 2.16(1)), and thus the eigenvalue cannot be 0.
    2. There exists at least one polynomial in \(\mathring{L}_n^d\) whose Hessian has exactly one positive eigenvalue. In fact, we showed that non-zero stable polynomials with non-negative coefficients have Hessians with exactly one positive eigenvalue (Prop 2.14).
